During the fifth week, I created a document containing the signal plots, statistical results, and boxplots produced during the previous stages of the project. I then used these figures to analyze the physiological data and consider the differences between pain and no-pain conditions.

The features showed noticeable variability, although the amount of variability differed depending on the physiological signal. Some signals remained within a relatively narrow range, while others showed larger fluctuations and wider distributions.

The signal with the greatest visible variability could be identified by comparing the signal plots, variance values, and boxplots. Signals with larger changes over time, higher variance, and wider boxplots demonstrated greater variability.

I also reviewed the paper, “Multimodal Fusion of Physiological Signals and Facial Action Units for Pain Recognition,” by Hinduja et al. The paper suggests that physiological signals and facial movements may provide complementary information for pain recognition. Physiological signals can reflect internal bodily responses, while facial action units represent visible muscular movements associated with expressions.

Combining these forms of information may improve pain recognition because one source can provide information that the other source may miss. However, facial expressions can vary between individuals, and some people may hide or suppress visible signs of pain. Physiological signals can also be affected by stress, movement, environmental conditions, or individual differences. For this reason, multimodal pain-recognition systems should be evaluated across diverse participants and conditions before being used in real-world settings.
